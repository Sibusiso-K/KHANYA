\begin{abstract}
\noindent South Africa mined about 71\% of the world's platinum in 2025, yet its concentrators still set feed rate, grind and reagents before the laboratory can say what was in the ore. REEFPRINT/KHANYA reads the ore at three speeds and turns what it reads into one decision. On the belt, a visible and short-wave infrared (VNIR--SWIR) hyperspectral camera feeds a model at the site that predicts the ore's Bond work index with a one-sided 90\% conformal upper bound, in 92\,ms per parcel on a CPU. On 146 public drill-core composites, the model explains 48\% of the variance in work index out of fold ($R^2$ = 0.479, against 0.210 for an ordinary colour camera and $-0.03$ for the average), and its 80\% intervals cover 79.5\% of samples. Setting the mill feed from the bound gave a simulated throughput gain of +1.9\% (95\% CI +0.9 to +2.9\%), with overloads non-inferior within a pre-registered margin. In the laboratory, a reflected-light microscope layer identifies pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite and pentlandite (IoU 0.8695, 0.5755 and 0.5468 on 12 held-out sections; mean IoU 0.5725 pooled, 0.4671 per section; magnetite is not detected) and made no unsafe recommendation in 12 sections. Every proposal passes explicit gates and waits for a person to approve it before the ore reaches the mill; otherwise the site's conservative setting applies, and the decision is recorded in a hash-chained, post-quantum-signed log. All results come from public data replayed offline: no South African belt has been scanned, and the plant is simulated. We ask Mintek and a producer for a nine-month pilot: one belt, one truth laboratory, paid on measured value.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hyperspectral imaging; Bond work index; conformal prediction; process mineralogy; PGM concentrators; advisory process control

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem, and why it is South Africa's}

The brief states the problem plainly: ``without real-time feedback, processing plants cannot adjust to changes in ore quality, leading to inefficient chemical usage and lower mineral yields''. Ore reaches the mill within minutes of leaving the belt. Assays and automated mineralogy, such as the QEMSCAN work of Mintek's own Minerals Processing and Characterisation Cluster \cite{moodley2026}, are batch laboratory work that reports after that ore has been processed. The plant runs three shifts a day, so each shift sets the mill and the flotation circuit for ore whose mineralogy it has not yet seen.

The cost of deciding blind falls most heavily on South Africa. In 2025 it mined an estimated 120,000\,kg of the world's 170,000\,kg of platinum (about 71\%) and holds about 63,000 of the world's more than 76,000 tonnes of PGM reserves (about 83\%), mostly in the Bushveld Complex \cite{usgs2026}. The same source attributes the 9\% fall in South African PGM output in 2025 to lower palladium prices, higher deep-level mining costs and ongoing disruptions to the electricity supply. Zimbabwe, the third-largest platinum producer (18,000\,kg in 2025), mines the same family of ores on the Great Dyke \cite{usgs2026}.

Recovery is where the money is. UG2 ore carries about 30\% \cro{} against about 0.1\% for Merensky ore, and the smelter caps the chromite in concentrate. Mintek testwork made UG2 concentrate at 87\% recovery with 2.9\% \cro{}, and showed that grades and recoveries above 90\% were possible only if the \cro{} limit was relaxed \cite{jones2005}. Producers report recovery moves of this size to investors: Valterra improved concentrator recoveries by 1--2 percentage points at Mototolo and Amandelbult in 2025 \cite{valterra2025}. Comminution, which sets liberation and mill throughput, used 52\% of site electricity in a benchmark of gold and copper mines \cite{ballantyne2012}, on a South African grid that is itself a constraint.

\subsection{What existing instruments cannot tell the shift}

Plants already run many on-line sensors (Table~\ref{tab:instruments}). Each measures one property in one place. Advanced process control, including Mintek's MillStar and FloatStar, stabilises the circuit from process measurements. What is missing is an \emph{ore-side, uncertainty-aware input} to that layer: one that arrives before the ore reaches the mill, states how wrong it might be, and refuses to advise when its evidence is thin. A belt-mounted hyperspectral camera can supply part of that input. It carries no radioactive source, unlike isotope-source neutron-activation analysers, and it sees the ore before the mill does. It does not see sulphide minerals or PGMs, which is why a laboratory truth layer stays in the design.

\subsection{Contribution and scope}

The system reads the ore at three speeds and turns all three into one decision (Section~\ref{sec:arch}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{the belt} (REEFPRINT): a hyperspectral model predicts each parcel's grinding hardness with an upper bound, and Bond's law turns the bound into a mill feed-rate proposal;
\item \textbf{the laboratory} (KHANYA): a reflected-light microscope model identifies the sulphide phases and their liberation, the brief's three-phase deliverable;
\item \textbf{the decision}: a person approves each proposal within the ore's transit time, or the site's conservative setting applies; every step is signed and recorded.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Belt layer: hyperspectral hardness}\label{sec:beltmethod}

\emph{Sensor (design).} The design assumes a Specim SX25-class VNIR--SWIR line scanner above the belt \cite{specim}. At 162 lines per second on a belt running at 2\,m/s, the camera records a line every 12.3\,mm, with 1.88\,mm pixels across a 1.2\,m belt. It therefore averages over parcels of ore; it does not map individual particles.

\emph{Model.} Each parcel's spectra are resampled to fixed VNIR and SWIR grids and reduced to features by spectral region, including the OH/H\textsubscript{2}O, Al--OH and Mg--OH/CO\textsubscript{3} absorption regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:hsi}). For each target, a model family (linear or tree ensemble) is chosen by nested cross-validation within the training folds. All results are out of fold. Targets are kept only if they beat their strongest baseline under gates written before each run.

\emph{Bound and gate.} The feed rate is set from a one-sided 90\% split-conformal upper bound on \Wi{} \cite{lei2018}, not from the point prediction. An approximate CV+ bound \cite{barber2021} was tried first and failed its gate; the exact split-conformal bound, the plan's stated fallback, was then used, so the result is provisional. A distance gate calibrated on the training folds flags parcels unlike anything in training. Flagged parcels receive the conservative setting.

\subsection{Decision layer: from a bound to a plant setting}

A ball mill is usually limited by motor power. Bond's law gives the specific energy \cite{bond1961}
\begin{equation}
W = 10\,W_i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{80}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{80}}}\right)\ \text{kWh/t},
\end{equation}
so at a fixed grind target the achievable feed rate is proportional to $1/W_i$. The proposed feed is the design feed scaled by (design hardness) / (upper bound on \Wi{}). Power, feed size and grind target cancel, so no plant parameter is assumed. The proposal must sit inside a site-approved operating envelope; the demonstration envelope is 85--110\% of design feed, with 100\% as the conservative setting and ramp limits of $-10\%$/$+3\%$ per parcel (feed is cut fast and raised slowly). These values are stipulated for the demonstration; a site replaces every one of them.

A metallurgist may approve, modify within the envelope, reject or escalate. If nobody approves before the parcel reaches the mill (an assumed 90\,s transit), or the parcel is flagged, or the data are stale, the conservative setting applies and the case is escalated. The system never outputs ``unknown'' and never holds the last value. Every event is appended to a hash-chained record with Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 signed checkpoints \cite{fips204}. The microscope's advice reaches a simulated regrind parameter over OPC~UA \cite{iec62541}; the same tag map is designed for the feed rate.

\subsection{Laboratory layer: microscope phases}\label{sec:micromethod}

The microscope model is torchvision's DeepLabV3 with a ResNet-50 backbone \cite{chen2017,he2016}, trained for five classes on 8 epochs of 64 native-resolution 512\,px patches (batch 2, AdamW at $2\times10^{-4}$, cross-entropy; class-uniform patch sampling). The checkpoint under report has SHA-256 \sha{de7135a9\ldots}; the software refuses to command the simulator with any other checkpoint. A \emph{Quick} scan classifies six non-overlapping 512\,px fields (18.2\% of an S2 section); a \emph{full-section} scan uses overlapping tiles.

Connected regions of the predicted mask are split into grains. For S2, chalcopyrite and pentlandite are \emph{payload} and pyrrhotite is \emph{reject}. Each grain is \emph{free} if at least 50\% of its classified area is payload, \emph{locked} if less, or carries no valuable mineral; the 50\% floor follows the fall in composite-particle flotation recovery below about half surface exposure \cite{liberation911}. A rule-based advisor turns these measures into \emph{grind finer}, \emph{continue} or \emph{verify before acting}. Advice reaches the simulator only if the checkpoint is approved, the image is a validated sample that passes a colour-cast check, at least 9 payload-bearing particles were found, and mean confidence is at least 0.85. Three of the advisor's four thresholds are placeholders awaiting values from site metallurgists.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Belt models are scored out of fold against the strongest baseline (fold mean, fold median, and a model using only RGB colour), with gates fixed in a pre-registration committed before each run. The microscope uses the publisher's 12 held-out S2 sections, never used for training or selection. Its IoU is reported pooled over all 103,795,344 test pixels and averaged per section, with percentile bootstraps over sections (2,000 resamples, seed 42). Decision-level agreement runs the advisor on the model's mask and on the expert mask. A disagreement is \emph{unsafe} if the model says \emph{continue} where the expert-mask advice does not, or gives non-hedged advice where the expert mask says \emph{grind finer}.

\section{System architecture}\label{sec:arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} and Table~\ref{tab:speeds} summarise the system. The belt monitor (REEFPRINT Live) runs the hardness model, the decision view and the signed record. The microscope workbench (KHANYA) is a React and TypeScript application on a FastAPI service with PyTorch inference on the CPU; in local mode it accepts only loopback connections and makes no external network requests. Both run offline on one laptop, so a power or network outage does not stop the advice. An optional assistant may route questions, but no language model computes a measured or control value.

Three environments exist, and each claim names its environment. A static public demonstration runs the belt monitor on precomputed data, with an unsigned browser-sandbox record. The secure server (role-based access, scrypt password hashing, CSRF protection, rate limits, signed ledger) passed 17 of 17 security tests locally and has not been deployed publicly. The microscope workbench ran locally. \textbf{Software verification:} the workbench has 180 pytest functions and 7 browser tests, with four continuous-integration checks passing; the belt branch has 328 pytest functions, and static analysis (Bandit) reported 0 issues after a dependency audit led to an upgrade of \texttt{cryptography}.

\subsection{Belt: from hardness to a plant decision}

Figure~\ref{fig:decision} shows the decision for the same parcel as the operator sees it, and Table~\ref{tab:belt} replays four feed-rate policies over all 146 composites. Setting the feed from the bound, with the out-of-distribution fallback, gave a simulated throughput gain of +1.9\% (95\% CI +0.9 to +2.9\%). Overloaded parcels fell from 8.9\% to 6.8\%; the one-sided 95\% upper bound on the difference is +1.4\,pp, inside the pre-registered +3\,pp non-inferiority margin. Feeding from the point prediction instead would overload almost half the parcels, which is why the policy uses a bound. The policy captures 13\% of the gain that perfect information would give, so better hardness models have room to add value.

The result is provisional: the exact bound was adopted after the approximate bound failed, HIDSAG has no time order (so stale data, transit time, stockpile mixing and ramp limits are not simulated), and the ore is not Bushveld ore. A robustness test, pre-registered before it was run, found that a new capture of the same ore keeps its error (mean absolute error $\times$1.00, 95\% CI 0.99--1.01). Half the field of view ($\times$1.01), added noise ($\times$1.00) and $\pm$15\% illumination ($\times$1.01) were also tolerated. A $\times$1.25 wavelength drift went mostly unflagged, so a wavelength-calibration gate was added to the hardware design.

\subsection{Is it physically possible?}

Every claim was tested against its physics, and the failures stay in the report (Table~\ref{tab:physics}). The most useful negative check concerns load-shedding: re-ordering ore during electricity curtailment cannot create tonnes, because energy per tonne is conserved over a cycle, so that claim was rejected.

\subsection{Laboratory: decisions and grain evidence}

Running the advisor on the model's masks and on the expert masks, the shipped (topology-refined) pipeline disagrees on 8 of 12 sections, and every disagreement is a request for verification: 0 unsafe and 0 conservative. \section{Discussion}

\subsection{What changes on a shift}

Two levers are covered, each with a named owner. The control room receives a hardness bound and a feed-rate proposal for each parcel before it reaches the mill. The metallurgist receives liberation evidence from the laboratory for grind and regrind decisions, and can audit it grain by grain. In both cases the system is an advisory input inside limits the site sets, beside existing process control such as MillStar and FloatStar; it is not a controller. Two design choices carry the safety argument: a bound rather than a point prediction (Table~\ref{tab:belt}), and a conservative fallback rather than the last value, because the last value may itself be wrong.

\subsection{Why this matters to Mintek, and where it can go}

Mintek's mandate is ``to promote mineral technology and foster the establishment and expansion of industries in the field of minerals and their derived products through research, development, and technology transfer'' \cite{mintek2025}. The system fits that mandate in four ways, each drawn from Mintek's own published work.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{More truth-laboratory work, not less.} The models need QEMSCAN composites and Bond tests to retrain and audit them. Mintek's Minerals Processing and Characterisation Cluster runs QEMSCAN \cite{moodley2026}; Mintek already characterises drill core mineralogically for chromite and iron-ore washability, and applies ore characterisation to flotation reagent selection for PGMs and base-metal sulphides \cite{mintek2025}.
\item \textbf{A feed-forward input to Mintek's process control.} Mintek reports a 0.5\% throughput gain at a lead--zinc concentrator from AI-driven steady-state optimisation, worth over US\$2 million a year \cite{mintek2025}. An ore-hardness bound that arrives before the ore is the kind of input such optimisation can use.
\item \textbf{A business model Mintek has already proven.} Mintek's Process Control Implementation team's benefit-sharing model with Hindustan Zinc (Vedanta) in India links revenue to system performance and has generated over R35 million since 2021 \cite{mintek2025}. Our proposed pilot is priced the same way: paid on measured value.
\item \textbf{A route beyond South Africa.} Mintek's products and services are used in over 60 countries, its instruments group operates in 42, and it works through distributors including Molycop and Mercantil \cite{mintek2025}. Its footprint map includes Zimbabwe, the second PGM province of the region, and Zambia, the DRC and Chile, major copper-producing countries; the belt model was developed on Chilean copper ore. Mintek's flotation group aims to become a globally recognised centre of excellence in flotation research through technology partnership \cite{creamer2026}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No South African ore has been evaluated.} The belt data are Chilean Cu--Mo core and the microscope data are Norilsk ore.
\item The belt result is simulated on 146 composites replayed as parcels, without time order; it is provisional until a pilot measures it.
\item The belt camera does not identify minerals; it predicts hardness. Chromite and the silicates have not been trained on by the microscope, and magnetite is never detected.
\item The 12 held-out microscope sections were reused during development and may not be independent specimens; the training recipe is not reproducible.
\item The microscope's advice is sensitive to illumination, and three advisor thresholds are unsourced.
\item Liberation is measured in 2D and is an upper bound; in Quick mode most grains are cut by field borders.
\item Latency is CPU-only with small $n$. The plant interaction is a simulation; no claim is made about recovery, reagents or turnaround at a plant.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Indicative value}\label{sec:value}

No plant benefit has been measured, so value is shown as a worked example with labelled assumptions (Appendix~\ref{app:assumptions}). Northam's Zondereinde mine produced 333,050 equivalent refined 4E ounces in F2026 \cite{northam2026}. One per cent more recovered metal at that output is 3,331 ounces a year. At Valterra's realised basket prices of R32,611 per PGM ounce (2025 average) and R45,993 (first half of 2026) \cite{valterra2025,valterra2026}, that is R108.6\,M to R153.2\,M a year in revenue, before any incremental processing or refining cost. Against an assumed belt installation of US\$0.5--3.0\,M with US\$0.1--0.6\,M a year in running costs (five years at 10\%, R17.61 per US\$), the pilot breaks even at 0.03--0.23\% more recovered metal. These are scenarios, not forecasts; the pilot exists to measure the real number.

\subsection{The investment case}

The pilot needs to find a small effect to pay back. At Zondereinde's F2026 output, each extra 1\% of recovered metal is worth R108.6--153.2\,M a year in revenue (Section~\ref{sec:value}). Across an assumed installed cost of US\$0.5--3.0\,M and running cost of US\$0.1--0.6\,M a year, break-even is 0.03--0.23\% more recovered metal. For scale, Valterra reported concentrator recovery gains of 1--2 percentage points in 2025 \cite{valterra2025}, and Mintek's own AI optimisation raised a concentrator's throughput by 0.5\%, worth over US\$2 million a year \cite{mintek2025}. The break-even is a small fraction of moves the industry already reports.

The commercial structure keeps the producer's risk bounded. Its maximum exposure is the pilot's installed and running cost, with three exits on the way. After the pilot, the fee is a share of measured value, so it is zero if no value is measured. Mintek already earns this way: its benefit-sharing model with Hindustan Zinc has generated over R35 million since 2021 \cite{mintek2025}.

\section{Conclusions}

A hyperspectral camera on the belt can predict grinding hardness well enough to steer a mill: on 146 public drill-core composites the model explained 48\% of the variance in Bond work index, more than twice what colour alone explains, and a feed-rate policy built on its upper bound gave a provisional simulated throughput gain of +1.9\% without more overloads. A reflected-light microscope model identified three sulphide phases on held-out sections, with magnetite as a stated failure, and gave no unsafe advice in 12 sections. Both feed one decision that a person approves and a signed record keeps. These results hold for public analogue ores and a simulated plant. Whether they transfer to South African ore is the question a nine-month pilot, with Mintek as the truth laboratory, is designed to answer.

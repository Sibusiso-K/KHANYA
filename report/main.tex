\section{Introduction}

As graduate engineers working on Mintek's Problem 3, our responsibility is to give a concentrator's shift team a reliable reading of its ore before that ore reaches the mill, and to turn the reading into a plant setting that a person can approve. This report sets out what we built, how we tested it, what it would cost and earn, and how a funded pilot would prove it on South African ore. It is written for metallurgists, mineralogists, process engineers and the people who fund their technology. Technical terms are defined in the glossary (Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}).

\subsection{Problem statement}

The brief states the problem plainly: ``without real-time feedback, processing plants cannot adjust to changes in ore quality, leading to inefficient chemical usage and lower mineral yields'' \cite{brief}. Ore reaches the mill within minutes of leaving the belt, whereas assays and automated mineralogy are batch laboratory work. Mintek's own Minerals Processing and Characterisation Cluster runs QEMSCAN automated mineralogy \cite{moodley2026}, and a commercial laboratory describes mineralogy as ``not an overnight assay service'' \cite{als}. Consequently, each shift sets the mill and the flotation circuit for ore whose mineralogy it has not yet seen.

The cost of deciding blind falls most heavily on South Africa. Figure~\ref{fig:world} illustrates world platinum-group metal (PGM) mine production in 2025, highlighting that South Africa mined an estimated 120 of the world's 170 tonnes of platinum (about 71\%) and 70 of 190 tonnes of palladium. It also holds 63,000 of the world's more than 76,000 tonnes of PGM reserves, mostly in the Bushveld Complex \cite{usgs2026}. The same source attributes the 9\% fall in South African PGM output in 2025 to lower palladium prices, higher deep-level mining costs and disruptions to the electricity supply.

Recovery is where the money is. UG2 ore carries about 30\% \cro{} against about 0.1\% for Merensky ore, and the smelter caps the chromite in concentrate. Mintek testwork made UG2 concentrate at 87\% recovery with 2.9\% \cro{}, and recoveries above 90\% were possible only if the \cro{} limit was relaxed \cite{jones2005}. Producers report recovery moves of this size to investors: Valterra raised concentrator recoveries by 1.2--1.7 percentage points at Mototolo and Amandelbult in 2025 \cite{valterra2025}. Comminution, which sets both liberation and mill throughput, used 52\% of site electricity in a benchmark of mines \cite{ballantyne2012}. Thus, the problem is to read the ore before the mill, state how uncertain the reading is, and turn it into a safe, approved setting at a cost a producer can recover.

\subsection{Our solution: three speeds, one decision}

REEFPRINT/KHANYA reads the ore at three speeds and turns all three into one decision (Section~\ref{sec:arch}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{the belt} (REEFPRINT), every 30 seconds: a short-wave infrared (SWIR) camera above the mill-feed conveyor feeds a model that predicts each parcel's grinding hardness with an upper bound, and Bond's law turns the bound into a mill feed-rate proposal;
\item \textbf{the laboratory} (KHANYA), once a shift: a microscope model identifies the sulphide minerals and how well they are liberated, which is the brief's three-phase deliverable;
\item \textbf{the decision}, within the ore's transit time: an operator approves each change before the ore reaches the mill, or the site's conservative setting applies, and every step is signed and recorded.
\end{enumerate}
It is important to note what is not claimed: performance on South African ore, a measured plant benefit, mineral identification by the belt camera, or identification of chromite and the silicates by the microscope. \section{How the system works}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data}

No South African belt scans or labelled Bushveld micrographs were available, so every model was trained and tested on public data (Table~\ref{tab:data}). HIDSAG supplies hyperspectral scans of Chilean porphyry copper--molybdenum drill core with laboratory Bond work-index values \cite{ehrenfeld2023}. LumenStone~S2, a Norilsk nickel--copper sulphide ore \cite{korshunov2025}, shares the mineral assemblage of Bushveld ores but not their abundance: sulphides cover 62.8\% of its sections by area, whereas Merensky ore contains up to about 3\% base-metal sulphides and UG2 less \cite{jones2005}. Nevertheless, it is the right teacher for the phases, because the base-metal sulphides in UG2 are predominantly pentlandite, chalcopyrite, pyrrhotite and pyrite \cite{jones2005}.

\subsection{The belt layer: hardness from the infrared}\label{sec:beltmethod}

\emph{Sensor (design).} The design uses a SWIR line scanner such as the Specim SX25 (960--2500\,nm, 640 pixels across, up to 162 lines a second) with a visible--near-infrared (VNIR) companion camera \cite{specim}. On a 1.2\,m belt moving at 2\,m/s it records a line every 12.3\,mm with 1.88\,mm pixels. It therefore measures parcels of ore, not individual particles. In the pilot design a \emph{parcel} is 30\,s of belt (about 60\,m, or 4,860 scan lines averaged into one spectrum); in the replay reported here, a parcel is one HIDSAG composite scan of 80$\times$117 pixels.

\emph{Model.} Each parcel's spectrum is reduced to features from the infrared absorption regions of clay, mica, chlorite and carbonate minerals (Fig.~\ref{fig:hsi}), and a model predicts the Bond work index (\Wi{}, kWh/t). Results are reported only for samples the model never saw (out of fold), and a result counted only if it beat simple baselines under pass marks written down before each run.

\emph{Bound and gate.} The plant setting uses an upper bound that the true hardness should stay below 9 times out of 10 (a one-sided 90\% split-conformal bound \cite{lei2018}), not the best guess. A more data-efficient bound \cite{barber2021} was tried first and failed its pass mark, so the exact bound that the plan named as its fallback is used, and the result is provisional. An out-of-distribution (OOD) check flags parcels unlike anything in training: refused parcels receive the conservative setting, and borderline ones the stricter of the bound and the conservative setting.

\subsection{The decision layer and its cadence}\label{sec:decision}

The decision layer turns the hardness bound into a mill setting that a person can approve. Ball mills are usually limited by motor power, so the tonnes a mill can grind depend on how hard the ore is. Bond's law gives the specific grinding energy \cite{bond1961}
\begin{equation}
W = 10\,W_i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{80}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{80}}}\right)\ \text{kWh/t},
\end{equation}
where $F_{80}$ and $P_{80}$ are the feed and product sizes (\textmu m) passed by 80\% of the material. At a fixed grind target the feed rate the mill can carry is proportional to $1/W_i$, so the proposal is
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{proposed}} = F_{\text{design}} \times \frac{W_{i,\text{design}}}{W_{i,\text{bound}}}.
\end{equation}
Power, feed size and grind target cancel, so no plant parameter has to be assumed. For example, a design hardness of 16.6\,kWh/t and a bound of 16.9\,kWh/t give 98.2\% of design feed. The proposal must sit inside an operating envelope set by the site's metallurgist; the demonstration envelope is 85--110\% of design feed, with 100\% as the conservative setting.

Figure~\ref{fig:cadence} illustrates the proposed cadence, highlighting that a person approves changes, not every computation. A new proposal is computed every 30\,s, but the operator sees it only when it differs from the running setpoint by more than a deadband (for example 2\% of design feed) set by the metallurgist; smaller changes are not made. The operator may approve, modify within the envelope, reject or escalate. If nobody approves before the parcel reaches the mill (an assumed 90\,s), or the parcel is flagged, or the data are stale, the conservative setting applies. The system never holds the last value, because the last value may itself be wrong. Feed may be cut by up to 10\% but raised by only 3\% per parcel. The shadow phase of the pilot measures how many proposals per hour this produces, and the advisory phase tests whether operators can keep up. Every event is written to a tamper-evident, hash-chained record with conventional (Ed25519) and post-quantum (ML-DSA-65 \cite{fips204}) signatures. The microscope's advice reaches a simulated regrind setting over OPC~UA, the industrial data-exchange standard \cite{opcua}; the same interface is designed for the feed rate.

\subsection{The laboratory layer: minerals under the microscope}\label{sec:micromethod}

The microscope model is a standard image-segmentation network (DeepLabV3 with a ResNet-50 backbone \cite{chen2017,he2016}) that labels every pixel of a reflected-light image as resin, pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, pentlandite or magnetite. Only the approved checkpoint (SHA-256 \sha{de7135a9}, full hash in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) may send advice to the simulator. A \emph{Quick} scan classifies six 512-pixel fields of a section; a \emph{full} scan covers the whole section.

Connected regions of the mask are split into grains. Chalcopyrite and pentlandite are the valuable (payload) phases, and pyrrhotite is rejected. A grain is \emph{free} if at least half its area is payload and \emph{locked} otherwise; the 50\% line is the boundary between low and high middlings in the liberation classes used by Mintek's characterisation group \cite{moodley2026}. A rule-based advisor turns these measures into \emph{grind finer}, \emph{continue} or \emph{verify before acting}. It answers \emph{verify} when the liberation estimate lies within $\pm$0.335 of the 50\% line, and it gives no recommendation with fewer than 9 payload-bearing grains or mean confidence below 0.85. The 50\% line is sourced; the $\pm$0.335 band was calibrated on the 12 test sections (Section~\ref{sec:grains}); the other thresholds are placeholders for site metallurgists to set.

\subsection{How we tested it}\label{sec:testing}

Belt models were scored out of fold against the strongest simple baseline (the fold mean, the fold median and a colour-only model), with pass marks committed to the repository before each run. The microscope was scored on the publisher's 12 held-out S2 sections. These sections were never used to train the network, but they were evaluated many times during development: to compare pipelines, to choose the approved checkpoint over a retrained candidate, and to calibrate the advisor's uncertainty band. Microscope results are therefore optimistic, and an independent, locality-disjoint test set is the first deliverable of the laboratory pilot. Accuracy is the intersection over union (IoU: the overlap between predicted and expert-labelled areas, from 0 to 1), pooled over 103.8 million pixels and averaged per section, with bootstrap confidence intervals over sections. Decision-level agreement runs the advisor on the model's mask and on the expert's mask; a disagreement is \emph{unsafe} if the model gives a confident instruction that the expert mask does not support, for example \emph{continue} where the expert mask says \emph{grind finer}.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the system, highlighting that both layers pass the same safety gates and the same human decision. The belt monitor (REEFPRINT Live) runs the hardness model, the decision view and the signed record. The microscope workbench (KHANYA) is a web application (React) on a Python service (FastAPI and PyTorch) that runs on an ordinary laptop processor. In local mode it accepts connections only from the same machine and makes no external requests, so a network outage at the plant does not stop the advice. No language model computes a measured value or a control value.

Speeds were measured on a four-core laptop processor: 92\,ms per parcel (median); 16.7--21.2\,s per six-field microscope scan on mains power (5 runs) and 30.8\,s on battery. A static public demonstration runs the belt monitor on precomputed data; a secure server passed 17 of 17 security tests locally and is not deployed. Software verification is in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

The result is provisional for three reasons: the exact bound was adopted after the more efficient one failed, HIDSAG has no time order (so transit time, stockpile mixing and the asymmetric ramp limit are not simulated), and the ore is not Bushveld ore. Symmetric ramp limits were tested over 200 random parcel orders: $\pm$10\% per parcel left overloads unchanged ($-0.3$ points), but $\pm$5\% added 2.1 points and $\pm$2\% added 4.7, so feed must be cut fast. A pre-registered robustness test found that a new capture of the same ore keeps its error ($\times$1.00), as do half the field of view, added noise and $\pm$15\% illumination; a wavelength drift raised the error by a factor of 1.25 unflagged, so a wavelength-calibration check was added to the design.

\subsection{Is it physically possible?}\label{sec:physics}

Every claim was tested against its physics, and the failures stay in the report. Bond's law gives the throughput gain above (provisional). Grinding faster costs at most 0.25 percentage points of flotation recovery on the steep part of the recovery curve and none on the plateau (modelled), so residence time must be checked first. Re-ordering ore during load-shedding cannot create tonnes, because energy per tonne is conserved over a cycle: it gave $-3.8\%$ in simulation, and even perfect knowledge gave $-3.0\%$, so that claim was rejected. The camera's 12.3\,mm lines and 1.88\,mm pixels resolve parcels, not particles; no test showed that the belt camera can identify minerals, so the microscope carries the phases; and the system is optical only, with no radioactive source and no radiation sensing.

\section{Validation against literature and limitations}

\subsection{Validation against literature}

The results align with published work and with what producers report. Notole et al.\ \cite{notole2025} validated hyperspectral imaging and portable XRF against reference methods on Merensky Reef drill core scanned at the Council for Geoscience in Pretoria, so the sensing layer has been shown to work on Bushveld material, although not yet for hardness. Our 50\% free/locked line sits on the boundary between low and high middlings in Mintek's liberation classes \cite{moodley2026}, and the three sulphides the microscope identifies are the main base-metal sulphides of UG2 ore \cite{jones2005}. On the plant side, Mintek's AI optimisation raised a lead--zinc concentrator's throughput by 0.5\%, worth over US\$2 million (about R33\,million) a year \cite{mintek2025}, so throughput moves of this order are both achievable and valued.

Four ideas were tested and withdrawn, and they mark where the method stops. The belt spectra did not predict QEMSCAN mineralogy once size fraction and process line were accounted for. Belt chemistry routed Bushveld PGE ore worse than the mine plan's seam label (balanced accuracy 0.79 against 0.82). The belt did not predict recovery, lime or pH better than a baseline. Plant tags never forecast concentrate silica an hour ahead better than the last laboratory assay.

\subsection{Limitations}

The following limitations apply: (a) no South African ore has been evaluated, and a model trained on Chilean porphyry core may not transfer, because hardness also depends on silicification, grain size and fracturing; (b) the belt result is simulated on 146 composites without time order, and without drill-hole identifiers the split is by sample, so it may be optimistic; (c) the belt camera predicts hardness and does not identify minerals, while the microscope has not been trained on chromite or the silicates; (d) the 12 microscope test sections guided development, and the approved checkpoint cannot be regenerated exactly; (e) the advisor's band was set on the test sections, its other thresholds are placeholders, and liberation is measured in 2D, which overstates it; and (f) latency is measured on one laptop, and the plant is a simulator. Compared with the abstract Mintek accepted, three things changed: the five Bushveld phases became three sulphides on an analogue ore, because no labelled Bushveld images existed; the QEMSCAN ``teacher'' became the pilot's truth-laboratory stream; and the fallback was reversed from holding the last setpoint to applying the conservative setting.

\subsection{Value proposition and business model}

Our value proposition is simple: the plant gets a reading of its ore before the mill, with a stated bound, and after the pilot it pays only for value that an independent party has measured. The buyer is the concentrator manager or general manager for metallurgy at a South African PGM producer, who owns recovery, concentrate quality and mill throughput; producers such as Valterra, Implats, Sibanye-Stillwater, Northam and Tharisa are market segments, not customers. Figure~\ref{fig:business} illustrates how money and value flow, highlighting the two kinds of payment: the pilot is paid at cost, and only the later fee depends on measured value. The Technology Innovation Agency (TIA), the national agency that funds technology development \cite{tia}, is the proposed bridge funder.

The revenue model has four streams: a paid laboratory pilot; a monthly licence for the laboratory software; recovery of the belt pilot's cost; and, after a successful pilot, at most 20\% of the measured net value per concentrator per year. The model suits Mintek for four reasons, each drawn from its own published work \cite{mintek2025}: (i) it creates more truth-laboratory work, because the models need QEMSCAN composites and Bond tests to retrain and audit them; (ii) it is a feed-forward input that Mintek's process control can use, as its AI optimisation already does with process data; (iii) Mintek has already proven value-linked pricing, since its benefit-sharing model with Hindustan Zinc in India has generated over R35\,million since 2021; and (iv) it has a route beyond South Africa, because Mintek's products and services are used in over 60 countries, its instruments group operates in 42, and it works through distributors including Molycop and Mercantil. The timing also favours a pilot now: South African PGM output fell 9\% in 2025 \cite{usgs2026}, while Valterra's realised basket price rose from R32,611 per ounce in 2025 to R45,993 in the first half of 2026 \cite{valterra2025,valterra2026}.

\subsection{From throughput to recovered metal}\label{sec:value}

The belt result is a throughput gain, and revenue comes from recovered metal, so the two must be bridged. For a plant whose mill limits production, with unchanged head grade, the extra metal recovered is approximately
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta M}{M} \approx \frac{\Delta T}{T} + \frac{\Delta R}{R},
\end{equation}
where $T$ is throughput and $R$ is recovery. Grinding faster can cost recovery: the modelled flotation cost is up to 0.25 percentage points on the steep part of the recovery curve and none on the plateau (Section~\ref{sec:physics}), which at an 87\% recovery \cite{jones2005} is $\Delta R/R = -0.29\%$. Where the plant is limited by ore supply instead, extra throughput is worth nothing. Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} turns these cases into value at Zondereinde's output.

\subsection{Financial methodology}\label{sec:finance}

The following assumptions apply: (a) annual output is that of Northam's Zondereinde mine, 333,050 equivalent refined 4E ounces in F2026 \cite{northam2026}; (b) prices are Valterra's realised basket prices of R32,611 per ounce (2025) and R45,993 (first half of 2026), with cash operating costs of R19,488 and R20,677 per ounce \cite{valterra2025,valterra2026}, used as proxies; (c) costs are the ranges in Table~\ref{tab:pricing}; and (d) the pilot equipment has a 5-year life at a 10\% discount rate. The annual value of an uplift $u$ in recovered metal is
\begin{equation}
V = u \times Q \times m ,
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is annual output in ounces and $m$ the margin per ounce. For $u$ = 1\% and $m$ = price less cash cost,
\begin{equation}
V = 0.01 \times 333{,}050 \times (\text{R}13{,}123 \text{ to } \text{R}25{,}316),
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to R43.7--84.3\,million a year (R108.6--153.2\,million in revenue before costs). Deducting the full average cash cost is conservative, because extra ounces from an existing mill cost less than the average ounce. The installed cost is spread over the equipment's life with the capital recovery factor
\begin{equation}
\text{CRF} = \frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n-1} = 0.2638 \quad (r = 10\%,\ n = 5),
\end{equation}
so the annualised cost is
\begin{equation}
C = \text{CRF} \times C_{\text{installed}} + C_{\text{running}},
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to R3.8\,million a year (low), R11.4\,million (middle) and R22.8\,million (high), or R25.4\,million for the high case with contingency. The break-even uplift is then $u_{\text{BE}} = C/(Q \times m)$: 0.05\% in the best case (low cost, 2026 margin) and 0.52\% in the worst (high cost, 2025 margin), or 0.58\% with contingency. Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven} illustrates the break-even, highlighting that even the worst case needs less than a third of the simulated gain.

\section{Implementation and support}\label{sec:implementation}

The pilot depends on people more than on hardware. Appendix~\ref{app:impl} gives the detailed plans; this section summarises them.

\emph{Support (Table~\ref{tab:support}).} The host site provides a sponsor, a process metallurgist, an analytical chemist, a process control engineer, an instrumentation technician and an OT security officer; Mintek provides a mineralogist and MOTT; Team Sonar provides three full-time roles.

\emph{Maintenance (Table~\ref{tab:maintenance}).} Most checks are automatic, and the system refuses rather than guesses when a check fails, so poor maintenance shows up as more conservative settings, not as wrong advice.

\emph{Compliance (Table~\ref{tab:compliance}).} The Mine Health and Safety Act requires a risk assessment with the health and safety committee and training before significant changes to plant or equipment \cite{mhsa}; POPIA governs approvers' names in the record \cite{popia}; Mining Charter clause 2.3.1 counts skills and R\&D spend \cite{charter2018}; IP from public funding falls under the IPR Act \cite{ipract}; Mintek and TIA spend under the PFMA \cite{pfma}; and the camera needs no radiation licence. Before installation, the health and safety committee, the management-of-change review, the IT/OT change board and the capital committee sign off.

\emph{Independent fact-checkers to outsource:} (a) a SANAS-accredited laboratory for Bond tests and assays \cite{sanas}; (b) Mintek's QEMSCAN for mineralogy; (c) an independent auditor for the value on which fees are charged, using IPMVP \cite{ipmvp}; (d) an IEC 62443 assessor for operational-technology security \cite{iec62443}; (e) an IP attorney through MOTT; and (f) a university reviewer for the model audit.

\emph{Training (Table~\ref{tab:training}).} Each group receives a short module before its phase starts, aligned with the site's workplace skills plan, which mines report to the Mining Qualifications Authority \cite{mqa}.

\section{Conclusions and recommendation}

The technical verdict is promising but unproven on South African ore. On public data, a belt hyperspectral model predicted grinding hardness well enough to steer a mill (simulated +1.7--1.9\% throughput, no more overloads), and a microscope model identified the three main base-metal sulphides of UG2 ore without unsafe advice, although its test sections also guided development. The financial verdict does not depend on large effects: even after cash costs and in the worst cost case, one belt breaks even at about half a per cent more recovered metal. We therefore recommend that TIA, Mintek and one PGM producer approve a nine-month pilot, one belt and one truth laboratory, with gates at months 2, 5 and 9. The single most important uncertainty, whether the hardness model transfers to Bushveld ore, is answered by month 5.

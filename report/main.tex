\begin{abstract}
\noindent South African concentrators set mill feed, grind and reagents before the laboratory can say what was in the ore. REEFPRINT/KHANYA is a decision-support system that gives the shift team that reading earlier, at two speeds, and leaves the decision with a person. In the proposed belt deployment, a short-wave infrared camera would score each 30-second parcel of mill feed for grinding hardness with a conservative upper bound, and Bond's law would turn the bound into a proposed feed rate inside limits set by the metallurgist; so far the hardness model has been tested only by replaying public drill-core data. The intended product extends belt sensing to parcel-level mineral-composition estimates matched to laboratory mineralogy; current evidence for this is exploratory copper-data regression and local inference software on recorded spectra, so belt PGM mineralogy and its $\leq$10\% target remain unvalidated. In the shift laboratory, a microscope model maps pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite and pentlandite in polished sections and reports their areas with grain-by-grain evidence; the current method withholds operational advice. An operator would approve, modify or reject every proposed change, the plant's own control system would keep authority, and every step is signed and recorded. All results so far come from public analogue datasets and a plant simulator. On 146 public porphyry-ore composites the hardness model reached an out-of-fold $R^2$ of 0.479, its 90\% upper bound covered 93.2\% of samples, and a simulated feed-rate replay estimated a 1.7--1.9\% throughput gain over a fixed design-feed policy. The historically evaluated microscope checkpoint reached IoU 0.870, 0.576 and 0.547 for pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite and pentlandite on 12 sections reused during development; the audited application build uses a different checkpoint, which showed large abundance errors on one section. We propose a six-month pilot at one host concentrator with both layers on site, the belt sensor leased and mounted only after a bench test on host ore, costed at R10.69--13.15\,million and proposed to be shared between the Technology Innovation Agency (TIA) and the host. Its targets, not yet results, are a phase-abundance error of $\leq$10\% on at least 90\% of 30 locked microscope specimens, the same target for belt mineral estimates against laboratory mineralogy on locked belt composites, and a hardness bound that holds on the host's belt; if its gates pass, it aims to generate evidence for independent assessment toward technology readiness level (TRL) 5--6 for the microscope (now 4) and 6 for the belt system (now 3).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

As Team Sonar, working on Mintek's Problem 3, our responsibility is to give a concentrator's shift team a reliable reading of its ore before that ore reaches the mill, and to turn the reading into a plant setting that a person can approve. This report sets out what we built, how we tested it, what it would cost and earn, and, centrally, the six-month pilot that would test it on South African ore (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}). It describes a research prototype: the results are on public analogue data and a simulated plant. It is written for metallurgists, mineralogists, process engineers and the people who fund their technology. Technical terms are defined where first used; TRL is the standard technology readiness level, from 1 (basic principles) to 9 (proven in operation) \cite{trl}.

\subsection{Problem statement}

The brief states the problem plainly: ``without real-time feedback, processing plants cannot adjust to changes in ore quality, leading to inefficient chemical usage and lower mineral yields'' \cite{brief}. Ore reaches the mill within minutes of leaving the belt, whereas assays and automated mineralogy are batch laboratory work. Mintek's own Minerals Processing and Characterisation Cluster runs QEMSCAN automated mineralogy \cite{moodley2026}, and a commercial laboratory describes mineralogy as ``not an overnight assay service'' \cite{als}. Consequently, each shift sets the mill and the flotation circuit for ore whose mineralogy it has not yet seen.


The cost of deciding blind falls most heavily on South Africa, which mined an estimated 120 of the world's 170 tonnes of platinum in 2025 (about 71\%) and 70 of 190 tonnes of palladium. It also holds 63,000 of the world's more than 76,000 tonnes of PGM reserves, mostly in the Bushveld Complex \cite{usgs2026}. The same source attributes the 9\% fall in South African PGM output in 2025 to lower palladium prices, higher deep-level mining costs and disruptions to the electricity supply.

Recovery is where the money is. UG2 ore carries about 30\% \cro{} against about 0.1\% for Merensky ore, and the smelter caps the chromite in concentrate. Mintek testwork made UG2 concentrate at 87\% recovery with 2.9\% \cro{}, and recoveries above 90\% were possible only if the \cro{} limit was relaxed \cite{jones2005}. Producers report recovery moves of this size to investors: Valterra raised concentrator recoveries by 1.2--1.7 percentage points at Mototolo and Amandelbult in 2025 \cite{valterra2025}. Plants do not decide entirely blind: operators, advanced process control (APC) and the mine plan already carry information about the ore. Thus, the decision we aim to improve is narrower: setting mill feed for each parcel of ore before it arrives, with a stated uncertainty, at a cost a producer can recover.

\subsection{Proposed solution: three speeds, one decision}

REEFPRINT/KHANYA reads the ore at three speeds (Section~\ref{sec:arch}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{the belt} (REEFPRINT), every 30 seconds: a short-wave infrared (SWIR) camera above the mill-feed conveyor feeds a model that predicts each parcel's grinding hardness with an upper bound, and Bond's law turns the bound into a mill feed-rate proposal. today the belt layer estimates hardness; extending it to parcel mineral composition is the intended product and a pilot question (Section~\ref{sec:goals}), not a current capability;
\item \textbf{the laboratory} (KHANYA), once a shift: a microscope model classifies sulphide minerals in polished sections, which addresses the brief's three-phase requirement on slower, prepared samples;
\item \textbf{the decision}: an operator reviews each proposed change before the ore reaches the mill; the site's existing control system keeps authority, and every step is signed and recorded.
\end{enumerate}
It is important to note what is not claimed: real-time mineralogy on the belt, performance on South African ore, a measured plant benefit, or identification of chromite and the silicates. \section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data}

No South African belt scans or labelled Bushveld micrographs were available, so every model was trained and tested on public data; none was supplied by Mintek or a producer. HIDSAG (CC0) supplies visible (39-band) and SWIR (75-band) scans of 146 Chilean porphyry copper--molybdenum drill-core composites with laboratory Bond work-index values \cite{ehrenfeld2023}. A set of 1,112 Bushveld chromitite assay intervals (CC BY 4.0) was used to check PGE routing \cite{bachmann2019}. LumenStone~S2 (research use only; 49 micrographs, 37 for training and 12 publisher test sections reused in development), a Norilsk nickel--copper sulphide ore \cite{korshunov2025}, shares the mineral assemblage of Bushveld ores but not their abundance: sulphides cover 62.8\% of its sections by area, whereas Merensky ore contains up to about 3\% base-metal sulphides and UG2 less \cite{jones2005}. It supplies labels for the relevant phases, because the base-metal sulphides in UG2 are predominantly pentlandite, chalcopyrite, pyrrhotite and pyrite \cite{jones2005}; its much richer sulphide content and different preparation can still cause transfer failure, which only host specimens can test.


\subsection{The belt layer: hardness from the infrared}\label{sec:beltmethod}

\emph{Sensor (design).} The design uses a SWIR line scanner such as the Specim SX25 (960--2500\,nm, 640 pixels across; 162 lines a second assumed, to be confirmed for the configured unit) with a visible--near-infrared (VNIR) companion camera \cite{specim}. On a 1.2\,m belt at 2\,m/s it records a line every 12.3\,mm with 1.88\,mm pixels, so it measures parcels of ore, not particles. In the pilot design a \emph{parcel} is 30\,s of belt (about 60\,m, or 4,860 scan lines averaged into one spectrum); in the replay reported here, a parcel is one HIDSAG composite scan of 80$\times$117 pixels.

\emph{Model.} Each parcel's spectrum is reduced to features from the infrared absorption regions of clay, mica, chlorite and carbonate minerals (Fig.~\ref{fig:hsi}), and a model predicts the Bond work index (\Wi{}, kWh/t). Results are reported only for samples the model never saw (out of fold), and a result counted only if it beat simple baselines under pass marks committed to the repository before each run. Five targets and several model variants were examined and only the work index survived, so the result is exploratory selection, not a confirmatory test; HIDSAG has no drill-hole identifiers, so grouped independence of the folds cannot be verified.

\emph{Bound and gate.} The plant setting uses an upper bound that the true hardness should stay below 9 times out of 10 (a one-sided 90\% split-conformal bound \cite{lei2018}), not the best guess. A more data-efficient bound \cite{barber2021} was tried first and failed its pass mark, so the exact bound that the plan named as its fallback is used, and the result is provisional. The 90\% is a marginal coverage target that assumes new parcels resemble the calibration data; it is not a per-parcel guarantee and does not certify protection against overload. An out-of-distribution (OOD) check flags parcels unlike anything in training, and their advice is withdrawn.

\subsection{The decision layer and its cadence}\label{sec:decision}

The decision layer turns the hardness bound into a mill setting that a person can approve. Ball mills are usually limited by motor power, so the tonnes a mill can grind depend on how hard the ore is. Bond's law gives the specific grinding energy \cite{bond1961}
\begin{equation}
W = 10\,W_i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{80}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{80}}}\right)\ \text{kWh/t},
\end{equation}
where $F_{80}$ and $P_{80}$ are the feed and product sizes (\textmu m) passed by 80\% of the material. At a fixed grind target the feed rate the mill can carry is proportional to $1/W_i$, so the proposal is
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{proposed}} = F_{\text{design}} \times \frac{W_{i,\text{design}}}{W_{i,\text{bound}}}.
\end{equation}
The ratio removes explicit power and size terms only when power, feed size, product target and circuit efficiency are held constant; the pilot must test that approximation for the selected circuit. For example, a design hardness of 16.6\,kWh/t and a bound of 16.9\,kWh/t give 98.2\% of design feed, inside an envelope set by the site's metallurgist (85--110\% in the demonstration). The demonstration falls back to 100\% of design feed; at a site, the fallback is the host's own operating procedure.


In the proposed cadence a person approves changes, not every computation: a parcel is scored as soon as it is scanned, and the operator has the rest of an assumed 90\,s belt-to-mill transit to act. A proposal is computed every 30\,s (at most 120 an hour), but the operator sees it only when it differs from the running setpoint by more than a deadband set by the metallurgist (for example 2\% of design feed), and may approve, modify within the envelope, reject or escalate. With no approval in time, a flagged parcel or stale data, the advice is withdrawn and the site's own control policy continues; the system never raises feed on its own. Feed may be cut by up to 10\% but raised by only 3\% per parcel, and the shadow phase measures the real operator-facing rate before anyone acts. Every event is written to a hash-chained record with conventional (Ed25519) and post-quantum (ML-DSA-65 \cite{fips204}) signatures; this supports traceability, not the correctness of the measurement. In the historical experiments the microscope's advice reached a simulated regrind setting over OPC~UA \cite{opcua}; the current method abstains, and the feed-rate path is proposed, not built.

\subsection{The laboratory layer: minerals under the microscope}\label{sec:micromethod}

The microscope model is a standard image-segmentation network (DeepLabV3 with a ResNet-50 backbone \cite{chen2017,he2016}) that labels every pixel of a reflected-light image as resin, pyrrhotite, chalcopyrite, pentlandite or magnetite. In the historical experiments, only the evaluated checkpoint (SHA-256 \sha{de7135a9}; full hash in the evidence manifest, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) could send advice to the simulator. A \emph{Quick} scan classifies six 512-pixel fields of a section; a \emph{full} scan covers the whole section.

Connected regions of the mask are split into grains. For this demonstration, chalcopyrite and pentlandite are treated as payload and pyrrhotite as reject; that assignment is a demonstration assumption for this ore, because pyrrhotite's value depends on PGM deportment and the host's treatment objectives. In the historical experiments, a grain at least half payload by area was \emph{payload-dominant} (``free''), otherwise \emph{reject-dominant} (``locked''), a 2D proxy, not liberation. A rule-based advisor turned these measures into \emph{grind finer}, \emph{continue}, \emph{verify before acting} or \emph{adjust reagent}, as hypotheses for a metallurgist, answering \emph{verify} within $\pm$0.335 of the 50\% line (calibrated on the 12 evaluation sections, Section~\ref{sec:grains}) and abstaining below 9 payload-bearing grains or confidence 0.85. The current application (region-aware contract version~3, measurement \texttt{observed-target-support-v3}) reports target-area categories instead, records retained target support and each exclusion cause, does not apply the historical margin, and always abstains: operational advice remains withheld pending method validation, applicable calibration, release review and host approval.

\subsection{Evaluation method}\label{sec:testing}

Belt models were scored out of fold against the strongest simple baseline (the fold mean, the fold median and a colour-only model), with pass marks committed to the repository before each run. The microscope was scored on the publisher's 12 S2 test sections. They were excluded from network training but reused during development, to compare pipelines, select the checkpoint and calibrate the advisor band, so we report them as \emph{development-influenced evaluation sections}, not an independent test. Accuracy is the intersection over union (IoU: the overlap between predicted and expert-labelled areas, from 0 to 1), pooled over 103.8 million pixels and averaged per section, with bootstrap confidence intervals over sections. Decision-level agreement runs the same advisor on the model's mask and on the expert's mask; a disagreement is \emph{unsafe} if the model gives a confident instruction that the expert mask does not support. This measures sensitivity to segmentation error, not whether the rules themselves are metallurgically right.

\subsection{Validation targets and independent protocol (proposed)}\label{sec:protocol}

The $\leq$10\% error target must be defined before samples are collected, because absolute and relative errors differ: a reference of 2\,wt\% predicted as 3\,wt\% is a 1-point absolute error but a 50\% relative error. We propose that the mineralogist and a statistician sign an acceptance contract naming the decision-critical phases, an absolute tolerance below an agreed quantification limit and a $\leq$10\% relative target above it, applied per specimen to every decision-critical phase (our proposed definition, Table~\ref{tab:goals}). Image-area abundance is judged separately from decision calibration and from physical liberation. Microscope image fractions are 2D areas, whereas QEMSCAN modal results are usually mass fractions, and a density conversion alone does not make a polished surface represent bulk mass. So the comparison unit, aggregation and density assumptions are fixed in advance, and a same-surface area comparison comes before any mass estimate.

A blind test needs more than hiding answers: a separate custodian holds specimen identities and laboratory results; the frozen model receives only acquisition inputs and permitted metadata; predictions are hashed before the reference is revealed; splits are grouped by specimen and collection event; and every refusal stays in the denominator. We propose 30 development, 10 calibration and at least 30 locked specimens, subject to a power calculation and quotes; if a model fails, it is fixed on development material and must face a new untouched cohort. As of 7 October 2026 this protocol is drafted, not signed; Section~\ref{sec:goals} gives the pilot's proposed targets.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the system, highlighting that both branches share provenance and operator controls but have their own quality checks. The belt and laboratory layers run on different time scales: laboratory results give slower context and audit evidence, not a check on each 30\,s parcel. The belt monitor (REEFPRINT Live) runs the hardness model, the decision view and the signed record. The microscope workbench (KHANYA) is a web application (React) on a Python service (FastAPI and PyTorch) that runs on an ordinary laptop processor. In local mode it accepts connections only from the same machine and makes no external requests, so local image analysis and evidence review can continue offline in the verified local configuration. No language model computes a measured value or a control value. Each result records the input and checkpoint hashes, a versioned measurement recipe, the sampled-field coordinates and the share of the image actually observed, and is refused if any of these goes stale, so every number can be traced to what produced it.

The historical 92\,ms median is a Kaggle CPU scoring benchmark on already prepared input, not camera-to-decision latency, which is unverified. Microscope timings for the benchmark checkpoint are log-reported, without retained raw records: 16.7--21.2\,s per six-field scan on mains power (5 runs) and 30.8\,s on battery; a fresh six-field scan in the audited build (\sha{fb78727d}) took about 81\,s on the audit machine, so the release chosen for the pilot will be re-benchmarked. A static public demonstration runs the belt monitor on precomputed data; a secure server passed 17 of 17 security tests locally and is not deployed. Software verification is in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\subsection{Belt: from hardness to a plant decision}\label{sec:beltdecision}



Four feed-rate policies were replayed over all 146 composites against a fixed design-feed policy (the 90th-percentile hardness), to show the trade-off between throughput and overloads (simulated; not PGM ore, not a time series). In the replay a parcel is \emph{overloaded} when its true hardness exceeds the hardness the feed was set for; this is a model quantity, not a plant trip. Setting the feed from the bound, with OOD parcels withdrawn, gave a simulated throughput gain of +1.9\% (95\% CI +0.9 to +2.9\%), and the overload share was 6.8\% against 8.9\%; the one-sided 95\% upper bound on the difference (+1.4 points) is inside the +3-point margin in the evaluation plan. The original replay did not apply the 85--110\% envelope; a later analysis with it gives +1.7\% (+0.7 to +2.7\%) and the same overload share. Feeding from the best guess instead would add 13.6\% but overload 45.2\% of parcels, which is why the policy uses a bound. Perfect knowledge would add 14.0\%; the bound's width is where the rest is lost, so a tighter bound from site data is the main route to a larger gain.


The result is provisional for three reasons: the exact bound was adopted after the more efficient one failed, HIDSAG has no time order (so transit time, stockpile mixing and the asymmetric ramp limit are not simulated), and the ore is not Bushveld ore. Over 200 random parcel orders, a $\pm$10\% ramp limit left overloads unchanged but $\pm$2\% added 4.7 points, so feed must be cut fast. A predefined robustness test found the error unchanged for a new capture, half the field of view, added noise and $\pm$15\% illumination, but 1.25 times larger, unflagged, under wavelength drift, so a wavelength-calibration check was added.

\subsection{Physical plausibility checks}\label{sec:physics}

In the evaluated flotation scenarios, grinding faster cost up to 0.25 percentage points of recovery on the steep part of the recovery curve and none on the plateau; this is the largest modelled loss, not a physical bound, so the site's curve must be measured. The camera resolves parcels, not particles; only exploratory copper-data regression (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) supports belt mineral estimates, so the microscope carries the phases until the pilot tests belt composition.

\section{Comparison with literature and limitations}

\subsection{Comparison with literature}

Published work supports the plausibility of the approach, not its validity here. Notole et al.\ \cite{notole2025} validated hyperspectral imaging and portable XRF against reference methods on Merensky Reef drill core scanned at the Council for Geoscience in Pretoria, with long-wave infrared as well as VNIR/SWIR sensing; that shows the sensing works on Bushveld material, not that our VNIR/SWIR hardness model does, and it is not a public paired conveyor dataset. The three sulphides the microscope classifies are the main base-metal sulphides of UG2 ore \cite{jones2005}, and our 50\% proxy line coincides with a boundary between middling classes in Mintek's liberation scheme \cite{moodley2026}, although that boundary does not by itself define free material.

Four ideas were tested and withdrawn, and they mark where the method stops. The belt spectra did not predict QEMSCAN mineralogy once size fraction and process line were accounted for; a grouped re-analysis (99 records, 36 composites) found only 23--91\% of eligible records within 10\% relative error, and a process-line baseline beat the spectral model for chalcopyrite and pyrite; an exploratory comparison on the same copper data (B02, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) narrowed but did not close that gap, so the current belt claim is limited to hardness and belt composition is a pilot question. Belt chemistry routed Bushveld PGE ore worse than the mine plan's seam label (balanced accuracy 0.79 against 0.82); the belt did not predict recovery, lime or pH; and plant tags never beat the last assay at forecasting concentrate silica.

\subsection{Limitations}

The following limitations apply, and (a), (b), (e) and (h) block deployment and performance commitments until tested, and justify a separately scoped feasibility investigation: (a) no South African ore has been evaluated, and a model trained on Chilean porphyry core may not transfer, because hardness also depends on silicification, grain size and fracturing; (b) the belt result is a replay of 146 composites without time order or drill-hole grouping, against a fixed design-feed policy rather than the plant's existing control, and a camera sees the belt surface, which may not represent the mass entering the mill; (c) belt mineral composition rests only on exploratory copper data, and the microscope has not been trained on chromite or the silicates; (d) the 12 microscope sections guided development, and the historical checkpoint cannot be regenerated exactly; (e) the advisor's rules (including the pyrrhotite-reject and 50\% assumptions) are unvalidated for any host ore, its band was set on the evaluation sections, and payload-dominance is a 2D proxy, not liberation; (f) only scoring latency is benchmarked, the plant is a simulator, and economic benefit has not been attributed at any site; and (g) there is no fresh paired PGM validation, the active application checkpoint differs from the evaluated one, chromite and silicate classes are missing, and reference-laboratory uncertainty is not yet quantified; and (h) the latest build withholds microscope advice and refuses the earlier counterexample, but inadequate support even in reference masks leaves specimen preparation, the component-as-particle assumption and calibration unresolved.

\section{Business model and financial case}\label{sec:business}


\subsection{Value proposition and business model}

Our value proposition is simple: the plant gets a reading of its ore before the mill, with a stated bound, and beyond a fixed support fee it pays a success fee only on value that an independent party has measured. No producer, Mintek or TIA has agreed to fund, host or license the work; the roles below are proposals. The buyer is the concentrator or metallurgy manager at a South African PGM producer, who owns recovery, concentrate quality and throughput; named producers are market segments, not customers. Money and value would flow as follows: TIA and the host fund the pilot at cost; Mintek earns from the reference laboratory work and licenses IP through its Office of Technology Transfer (MOTT); the producer receives hardness bounds, phase estimates and the signed record; and an independent auditor verifies the value on which a success fee, capped at 20\%, is charged. TIA, the national agency that funds technology towards commercialisation \cite{tia}, is the proposed main funder (Section~\ref{sec:tia}).

The proposed commercial structure has three parts: a capped validation contract; a service fee after acceptance; and an optional variable fee capped at 20\% of independently verified net value, with the baseline, measurement period, adjustments, negative-value treatment and audit rights defined in advance. The success fee is zero when verified net value is zero or negative; validation charges and the support fee are separate, so no blanket zero-fee promise is made. Willingness to pay is untested, and laboratory savings are not claimed. The model fits Mintek's published work \cite{mintek2025}: the models need laboratory work, and Mintek's benefit-sharing model with Hindustan Zinc has generated over R35\,million since 2021.

Plants already have cross-belt neutron analysers (bulk elements, with isotope sources that need a licence \cite{sahpra}), slurry analysers (grade after milling), automated mineralogy (phases, in days) and APC such as MillStar and FloatStar. The system replaces none of them; whether it adds ore-side information beyond APC, operator practice and mine-plan blending is what the pilot must show.

\subsection{From throughput to recovered metal}\label{sec:value}

The belt result is a throughput gain, and revenue comes from recovered metal, so the two must be bridged. For a consistent stream, metal output is proportional to dry tonnes $\times$ head grade $\times$ recovery, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta M}{M} = \Big(1+\frac{\Delta T}{T}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{\Delta G}{G}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{\Delta R}{R}\Big) - 1 ,
\end{equation}
where $T$ is throughput, $G$ head grade and $R$ recovery. With unchanged grade, a 1.75\% throughput gain and a recovery loss of 0.25 percentage points from 87\% (a relative change of $-0.29\%$) give 1.46\% more metal. Where the plant is limited by ore supply, extra throughput is worth nothing. If all of Zondereinde's output passed the instrumented circuit (an upper bound), 1.46\% more metal would be worth R64--123\,million a year in margin, and a quarter of that gain R16--31\,million; these are simulated gains, not measurements.



\subsection{Financial methodology}\label{sec:finance}

The following assumptions apply: (a) annual output is that of Northam's Zondereinde mine, 333,050 equivalent refined 4E ounces in F2026 \cite{northam2026}; (b) prices are Valterra's realised basket prices of R32,611 per ounce (2025) and R45,993 (first half of 2026), with cash operating costs of R19,488 and R20,677 per ounce \cite{valterra2025,valterra2026}, used as proxies for another company's mine, with different baskets, payability and cost structure; (c) for a commercial-life case, installed cost is the belt allowance without the six-month support line (US\$175,000--280,000, R2.87--4.59\,million) and running cost is the post-trial support (R0.49--0.98\,million a year); (d) the equipment has a 5-year life at a 10\% discount rate; and (e) an affected-output fraction $f$ of the mine's output passes the instrumented circuit, taken as 1 unless stated. The annual value of an uplift $u$ in recovered metal is
\begin{equation}
V = u \times f \times Q \times m ,
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is annual output in ounces and $m$ the margin per ounce. For $u$ = 1\% and $m$ = price less cash cost,
\begin{equation}
V = 0.01 \times 333{,}050 \times (\text{R}13{,}123 \text{ to } \text{R}25{,}316),
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to R43.7--84.3\,million a year (R108.6--153.2\,million in revenue before costs). Deducting the full average cash cost may be conservative where the mill has spare capacity, but extra mining, handling or bottleneck costs could change that, so a host-approved incremental cash-flow model is needed before investment. The installed cost is spread over the equipment's life with the capital recovery factor
$\text{CRF} = r(1+r)^n/[(1+r)^n-1] = 0.2638$ ($r = 10\%$, $n = 5$), so the annualised cost $C = \text{CRF} \times C_{\text{installed}} + C_{\text{running}}$, which is equivalent to R1.25--2.20\,million a year, or R2.50\,million with the 25\% contingency on the high case. The break-even uplift is then $u_{\text{BE}} = C/(f \times Q \times m)$. The more recovered metal needed is 0.015\% at low cost and 2026 margin with all output affected; 0.050\% at high cost and 2025 margin; 0.063\% if the 20\% fee is charged before system costs; 0.20\% if only a quarter of output is affected or the basket price falls 30\%; and 0.52\% on the superseded R49.2\,million installation grid. If the 20\% fee is charged on the margin before system costs, the producer breaks even only when $0.8\,V = C$. The break-even figures are far smaller than the precision with which a short plant trial can measure uplift, which is why measured value, not break-even, decides G5.



Annualising over five years describes continued successful use, not whether a six-month pilot pays for itself. The pilot cash view is simpler: R10.69--13.15\,million is a scoped planning allowance, not a maximum exposure, because VAT, duties, shutdown and civil costs are excluded and must be assigned to a payer. It is released in tranches (Table~\ref{tab:tranches}), so a stop at any gate leaves later tranches unspent; if the pilot runs to the end and the host declines to continue, the full allowance is spent, with equipment resale assumed to be zero in the pessimistic case. The fee basis must be agreed so that no support cost is charged twice, and measured value needs an agreed metallurgical accounting boundary and attribution method under IPMVP \cite{ipmvp}.

\section{The six-month pilot}\label{sec:pilot}

As Team Sonar, our pilot problem is this: in six months at one host concentrator, find out whether the microscope meets a $\leq$10\% phase-abundance target on independent local specimens, whether parcel-level belt estimates of mineral composition can meet the same target against matched laboratory mineralogy, and whether the hardness bound holds on the host's own belt, in shadow and then in a short supervised advisory window. Both layers run on site. A clear negative answer at any gate is a useful result, because it stops spending early.

\subsection{Pilot duration and feasibility}

The following assumptions make six months realistic: (a) contracting is finished at gate G0, including a quoted measurement-feasibility review of specimen preparation and image--reference correspondence, a leased sensor with a delivery date and a plant stop booked for weeks 8--10, so the clock starts at a readiness event; (b) the sensor is non-contact and mounts above an existing conveyor with read-only data, so installation takes days within that stop; (c) specimens come from the host's existing shift sampling and belt cuts, so 70 microscope specimens and 44 belt composites (each split for Bond and mineralogy references) over about 16 weeks need only about 5 and 3 a week; (d) reference results return in fortnightly batches within an agreed three weeks; and (e) the software, registry and abundance checks already exist and the build already abstains where support is inadequate, so month~1 goes on measurement feasibility, local data and development-only method selection; calibration is fitted later on its own cohort, and advice stays withheld pending validation, release review and host approval. No host, laboratory or sensor access is arranged yet, so these are G0 prerequisites, and $\leq$10\% remains a target. Two limits apply: the sensor is mounted only under the bench outcome matrix (Table~\ref{tab:benchmatrix}, week~8), and the four-week advisory window can show the workflow, timing and operator response, but cannot measure a gain of about 2\% with useful precision, so independently measured value (G5) needs a longer trial after the pilot. If supply, delivery or the plant stop slips, the pilot is extended; the cohort is never cut to keep the date. The added mineral work must be reflected at G0 in quotes, sample access, laboratory throughput and staff capacity.

\subsection{Pilot objectives and acceptance targets}\label{sec:goals}

Table~\ref{tab:goals} sets out the proposed targets, signed with the host before any evaluation specimen is collected. We use ``90\% accuracy'' in one strict sense for the microscope: at least 90\% of locked specimens must meet the error contract for every decision-critical phase. With 30 locked specimens, an observed 27 of 30 shows only that the true pass rate exceeds 76\% (one-sided 95\%); demonstrating 90\% itself needs 29 of 29, or 45 of 46 if one failure is allowed, and these bounds assume independent specimens. So 27 of 30 is a proposed observed acceptance rule, not proof of a 90\% pass rate; 46 specimens would add about R0.56\,million before contingency. On the belt, 24 composites give exploratory evidence of limited precision (22 of 24 within the contract bounds the pass rate only above 76\%), and the exact pass-count rule, handling of every critical phase, refusals and reporting limits are fixed before testing; the sampled cut must match the scanned parcel although the camera sees only the surface, and microscopy acceptance counts are never reused as belt evidence; and because a bound can cover 90\% simply by being too wide, its width and refusal rate are judged with its coverage. Beyond accuracy, the pilot first measures whether the estimates improve one named decision within its timing deadline, valued as incremental payable production or avoided cost against the baseline, minus operating, verification and error costs; reducing laboratory submissions would be a later, separately validated policy, not a consequence of a 10\% error target.

\subsection{Governance, compliance and training}

These are planned controls, not achieved compliance. The Mine Health and Safety Act requires a risk assessment with the health and safety committee, and training before significant changes to plant \cite{mhsa}, which applies to the belt installation; POPIA governs approvers' names in the record \cite{popia}; Mining Charter clause 2.3.1 counts skills and R\&D spend \cite{charter2018}; IP from public funding falls under the IPR Act \cite{ipract}, managed through MOTT under the hackathon agreement \cite{faq}, although the contract settles ownership; and Mintek and TIA spend under the PFMA \cite{pfma}. Independent checks would be commissioned, subject to agreement and method capability, from a laboratory accredited by SANAS for the selected method's scope \cite{sanas}, Mintek's QEMSCAN, a value auditor using a plan adapted from IPMVP \cite{ipmvp}, an IEC~62443 assessor, an IP attorney through MOTT and a university model reviewer. Each group is trained before its phase, aligned with the site's skills plan \cite{mqa}: laboratory staff and metallurgists before G1A work starts, instrument technicians at installation, and control-room operators before G4; competence is shown by observed tasks and sign-off, not attendance.

\section{Conclusions and recommendation}

The present contribution is a functioning, inspectable evidence workflow and a falsifiable route to local validation. On public analogue data the hardness model improves on simple baselines and the microscope classifies three relevant sulphide phases, but the feed-rate benefit is a simulation, the microscope results are development-influenced, the active application checkpoint has shown large abundance errors on one section, and the $\leq$10\% error target is untested. We therefore recommend the gated six-month, two-track pilot of Section~\ref{sec:pilot}, shared between TIA and the host. The $\leq$10\% local target has not been demonstrated; a commercial claim will depend on independent reference agreement, useful coverage and measured net value on the affected stream.
